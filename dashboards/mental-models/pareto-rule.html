The Pareto Rule

Also called the 80/20 rule: a small share of causes often produces most of the results.

The rule won't tell you the exact numbers. It tells you to measure first and sort second, because the few things that matter most are rarely obvious until the list is in order.

1. The idea

Effort and results are rarely shared out evenly. Often a few causes do most of the work, and the rest add a little each.

Analogy: look at your wardrobe. You probably reach for a few favourite pieces most days, while the rest of the rail waits for a special occasion.

What it says

Roughly 20% of the inputs produce roughly 80% of the outputs. The numbers vary from one situation to the next. The uneven shape is the point.

Where it came from

Economist Vilfredo Pareto found that about 80% of Italy's land belonged to about 20% of the people. Quality expert Joseph Juran later applied the idea widely and called it the "vital few and trivial many".

How to use it

List the items, measure each one, sort biggest first, and add up a running total. Start with the items that get you to your goal.

    It's a pattern, not a law

    Real lists might split 70/30 or 95/5, and some lists are nearly even. Use your measured numbers instead of assuming 80/20.

    The tail can change

    Today's small customer or rare bug can become tomorrow's big one. A ranking describes a moment, so measure again from time to time.

    Sorted isn't finished

    The ranking sets the order of work. Whether you can stop early depends on what the remaining items would cost you.
