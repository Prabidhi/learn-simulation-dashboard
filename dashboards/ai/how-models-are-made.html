How AI models are made: from raw text to your daily chat

Every frontier model goes through the same life: collect data, train billions of knobs to reduce a loss, teach it to be helpful, test it, freeze it, then serve it. Here you'll do every step with a model small enough to train in your browser.

1 · Data & tokens: what the model learns from

A model only knows what's in its training data. Labs gather enormous amounts of text (web pages, books, code, maths), clean it hard, then chop it into tokens.

Think of it like building a library and then cutting every book into Lego bricks. The cleaning decides which books go on the shelves; the tokenizer decides what the bricks look like.

2 · Knobs & loss: how "learning" actually works

A model is a function with adjustable numbers (parameters, or knobs). The loss measures how wrong its guesses are. Training repeatedly nudges every knob a little in the direction that lowers the loss. That's gradient descent.

Think of it like standing on a foggy hillside, trying to reach the lowest valley. You can't see the valley, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet. Take a step downhill, feel again, repeat. The learning rate is your step size.

The model's guess vs the data

This model has just 2 knobs: slope w and offset b. Red lines are the errors; the loss is their average squared length.

The loss landscape

Every point is one setting of the two knobs. Colour = loss (teal low, orange high). The ball is your model. Click anywhere to drop it there.
Try a learning rate near the maximum: the ball overshoots and the loss explodes. Too small and it crawls. A frontier model does exactly this, but with hundreds of billions of knobs at once. Calculus (backpropagation) works out the downhill direction for every knob in one sweep.

3 · Pretraining: train a real (tiny) language model, live

Pretraining is one job repeated trillions of times: read some text, guess the next token, measure the loss, nudge the knobs. Below is a genuine language model with fewer than 1,000 knobs that learns to predict the next character of a short text.

Think of it like a student reading the same library over and over. One full read is an epoch. At first the guesses are random; after enough passes, the student can finish sentences in the library's style. That's all a base model is: an extremely good next-token predictor.

The bottom dashed line is a ceiling on what this model can learn: it only looks at one previous character. Frontier models look back over thousands of tokens using the transformer's attention, and have billions of times more knobs, so their loss keeps dropping far lower. Same loop, vastly bigger.

4 · Post-training: from autocomplete to assistant

A pretrained base model just continues text. It doesn't know it's supposed to answer you. Post-training teaches it to follow instructions, be helpful and honest, and decline harmful requests.

Think of it like a brilliant graduate who has read everything but never had a job. Fine-tuning is an apprenticeship with worked examples of good answers. Reinforcement learning from feedback is a coach giving thumbs-up or thumbs-down on many attempts, so they learn what "good" looks like.

Same prompt, three stages

How do I reset my home router?

You are the feedback

Pick the better answer. Each choice becomes training data for a reward model, which then scores the model's attempts during reinforcement learning.
Modern models also get reinforcement learning on tasks where answers can be checked automatically (does the code pass its tests, is the maths answer right?), which rewards actually getting things correct, not just sounding good. Post-training doesn't add much knowledge; it shapes how the model uses what it already learned.

5 · Evaluate, freeze & ship

Before release, a candidate model is tested for capability, safety and regressions. The winning checkpoint is then frozen: its weights are saved to files, versioned, and never changed again. Improvements ship later as a new version.

Think of it like publishing a book. Final exams and an inspection come first. Then you print it: every copy is identical and the text is fixed. Readers use the book every day, but reading never changes the words on the page.

6 · Inference: what happens when you send a message

Your prompt becomes tokens. The frozen model computes a probability for every possible next token. One is picked, appended, and the loop runs again, one token at a time, until the answer is done.

Think of it like the world's best autocomplete, pressed over and over. Temperature controls how adventurous each pick is: low means "always the safest word", high means "surprise me".
your tokens→🔒 frozen model→probabilities→pick one→append↺ repeat
Next-token probabilities right now (top candidates):
Notice the 🔒. Your chats don't retrain the model in real time: the weights stay frozen while it answers. It "remembers" the conversation only because the whole conversation is fed back in as input each turn. Real serving adds tricks like caching earlier tokens' work (the KV cache) and batching many users onto the same GPUs.
