Speakers & amps: push, pipes and loudness

How sound is made, what an amplifier really does, what ohms and sensitivity mean, how to wire four speakers to a two-channel amp, why a "200 W" amp delivers about 30, and what changes when you move to a home-theater receiver. Everything below is live: drag, tap and watch the numbers.

1 · Sound is air being pushed back and forth

A speaker cone is a piston. It shoves the air forward and pulls it back, and that squeeze-and-stretch travels to your ear.

Analogy: ripples on a pond. How close together the ripples are is the pitch; how tall they are is the loudness.

2 · Push, pipes and loudness: one amp, one speaker

The amp makes a stronger copy of the music signal. Three numbers decide what comes out, and only one of them is yours to set.

Analogy: water. Volts are the pressure, impedance (ohms) is how narrow the pipe is, current is the flow, and sensitivity is how much sound the speaker makes from that flow, like miles per gallon.

3 · Wiring lab: four speakers on a two-channel amp

Two channels means two separate amps in one box, left and right. To run four speakers you put two on each channel, either in a chain (series) or side by side (parallel).

Analogy: series joins two pipes end to end, so the flow is squeezed twice and the ohms add up. Parallel lays two pipes side by side, so more flows and the ohms drop.

4 · The "200 W" label against real watts

The amp's push has a ceiling set by its power supply. Past it, the wave tops are cut flat. That is clipping, and it is where the harsh sound and the tweeter damage come from.

Analogy: a ceiling in a room. Jump as hard as you like; once your head touches it you go no higher. "200 W" is a car's brochure top speed: both channels added, lowest-ohm load, peak, downhill with a tailwind.

5 · What hurts a speaker

A speaker is a hair-thin coil of wire glued to a cone, sitting inside a magnet. That gives it exactly two weak points.

Heat

Most of the power becomes heat in the coil, not sound. Too much for too long and the wire or its glue burns, like a bulb filament. Tweeter coils are the thinnest, so they go first.

Travel

The cone hangs on a rubber edge that only stretches so far. Heavy bass shoves it past the limit and it tears or bangs the magnet, like a spring pulled too far.

Avoid

  • Playing through harsh, crunchy distortion
  • Heavy bass boost at high volume
  • Plugging cables with the amp on
  • Speaker wires touching each other
  • Feedback squeals and test tones, loud

Harmless

  • A small amp, kept below distortion
  • A big amp, at sensible volume
  • Series or parallel wiring itself
  • One speaker wired + and − reversed (thin bass, easy fix)

Warning signs: fuzzy or crackling sound, popping from the woofer, a hot smell. Turn it down straight away.

6 · From two channels to a receiver

A two-channel amp can only make a left and a right. A home-theater receiver has a separate amp for every speaker, plus the decoders that split a movie's sound into those channels. Compare today's series setup with a 5-channel receiver for each kind of source.

Analogy: a two-channel amp is a two-lane road. Wire four speakers to it and two cars share each lane, so the rear cars can only follow the front ones. A receiver gives every speaker its own lane and a traffic controller that sends each sound where it belongs.

9 · Moving over: rewire, then check a used receiver

Four steps take the speakers off the series chain and keep the tube sound for records.

1 · Un-series

Power everything off. Remove the jumper wires that chain each Sony behind a Polk, and disconnect all speaker wires from the Douk.

2 · One speaker per channel

On the receiver: Polk towers on Front L/R, Polk CS1 on Center, Sonys on Surround L/R. Red to red, black to black.

3 · Keep the tubes for vinyl

Turntable switched to LINE → Douk input → Douk's 3.5 mm line out → 3.5 mm-to-RCA cable → a receiver line input such as Media Player. Never the receiver's Phono input, and never the Douk's speaker terminals.

4 · Calibrate

Run the receiver's Audyssey setup with its microphone at your seat. It sets each speaker's distance, level and crossover.

10 · Challenges

Each one ticks itself when you manage it in the sections above.

✓

Chain reaction. In the wiring lab, make each channel see exactly 14 Ω.

Hint: in a chain, ohms add.

✓

Loud and clean. In section 4, get the Polk alone to 100 dB or more without clipping.

Hint: how many watts did that take? Fewer than you'd think.

✓

Too greedy. In the wiring lab, build a load under 3 Ω that would put the amp at risk.

Hint: lower both speakers' ohms and lay the pipes side by side.

✓

Find the voice. In section 6, give the center speaker something to say.

Hint: a two-channel amp has no center output.

✓

Keep the glow. In section 6, play a record through the tubes and the receiver together.

Hint: the Douk becomes a preamp.

✓

Fair fight. Wire both in parallel and make speaker B exactly as loud as speaker A.

Hint: in parallel both get the same push, so only one number separates them.

Formulas

watts = volts² ÷ ohms
series = R1 + R2
parallel = R1×R2 ÷ (R1+R2)
dB = sensitivity + 20·log(volts ÷ 2.83)

Rules of thumb

  • Double the power is only +3 dB
  • +10 dB sounds about twice as loud
  • Normal listening uses 1 to 5 W
  • In series the higher-ohm speaker takes the bigger share

Reading a label

  • Peak or "max" watts: a sprint
  • Continuous (RMS) watts: cruising speed
  • A speaker's watt rating is what it survives, not what it draws
